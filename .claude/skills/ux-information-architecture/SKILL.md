# UX & Information Architecture

## Outcome
Translate approved strategy into a structure visitors can understand and use. Every page, section and interaction must serve a visitor task or a business objective.

## Process
1. Read approved brief, strategy, constraints and available content.
2. Identify primary user groups from evidence, their tasks, context, questions, objections and accessibility needs.
3. Map journeys from entry point to successful task/conversion; include alternate paths and existing-client paths when real.
4. Define sitemap, navigation model, URL/page purpose and internal relationships.
5. Create section/page architecture with hierarchy and reason for inclusion. Do not begin with a generic template of hero/about/services/testimonials/FAQ.
6. Specify content needs, data, CTAs, forms, validation, loading/empty/error/success states and privacy implications.
7. Define responsive behavior for navigation, tables, tabs, galleries and complex components; do not treat mobile as a squeezed desktop.
8. Produce wireframes or annotated specifications proportional to project risk. For complex flows, prototype and validate before implementation.
9. Write acceptance criteria for each critical task and interaction.

## Interaction design
Use native HTML controls where appropriate. Prefer clear labels, visible focus, keyboard operation, understandable errors and predictable state. Avoid controls that look interactive but do nothing. Do not rely on hover-only behavior, color alone or drag-only gestures.

## Deliverables
Sitemap; journey/task map; page and section outline; component/interaction inventory; responsive behavior; content requirements; acceptance criteria; open questions.

## Review
Check that the architecture serves the primary task, reduces decision friction, supports assistive technology, fits available content and avoids unnecessary complexity.
