# Web Discovery & Strategy

## Outcome
Produce an evidence-based decision brief that makes clear who the website serves, what problem it solves, why the offer matters and what the visitor should do next.

## Process
1. Clarify business model, region, language, offer, service area, constraints and business objective.
2. Separate confirmed client facts, public evidence, hypotheses and unknowns.
3. Define research questions before searching. Prioritize primary sources, official business sources, direct competitors, customer language and credible market data.
4. Research a proportionate competitor set. Compare offer, audience, positioning, page structure, proof, CTA, content, UX and gaps; do not infer business performance from visual appearance.
5. Inspect the client's current digital presence and record concrete findings with URL/date. Avoid vague criticism such as "the site is outdated" without an observed reason.
6. Identify audience tasks, barriers, objections, trust requirements and decision criteria. Do not invent personas or demographic facts.
7. Define objective, primary audience, value proposition, differentiation supported by evidence, message hierarchy, CTA, conversion path and measurable indicators.
8. Recommend site type and scope based on actual business needs, budget, maintainability and content resources.
9. Deliver a compact research dossier: executive finding, facts/evidence, hypotheses, opportunity, risks, strategic direction, questions and sources.

## Reference discovery
Consult 2–5 relevant references initially. Search both the client's niche and transferable experiences such as editorial layout, navigation, booking, product discovery, conversion, motion or portfolio presentation. Read reference cards and attempt to inspect the originals. Record limitations honestly.

## Quality rules
- Browse current information when the question depends on current market, regulations, competitors, prices or product capability.
- Cite sources and dates. Prefer official/primary sources for facts; community sources can provide experience, not unquestioned truth.
- Do not fabricate customer research, search volumes, market size, competitor intent or conversion data.
- Distinguish visual opportunity from proven business result.
- Adapt recommendations to country, language, culture and professional rules.
- Do not recommend a feature simply because it looks impressive.

## Learning progression
For beginner tasks, explain how to inspect an online business and how to distinguish observation from inference. For advanced tasks, include positioning alternatives, evidence strength, trade-offs and measurable experiments.
